When a login belongs to one volunteer, your church can lose access to its email, website or money when that person moves on. A few simple habits prevent this and make your accounts harder to break into.

## List your important accounts

Make a list of the accounts your church relies on. Include:

- bank and giving accounts
- payroll
- email
- website and domain name
- social media
- online file storage
- your church management system

For each account, record what it's for, who manages it, and who else can get in if that person is unavailable. Don't write passwords on this list.

Ask a trustee to take responsibility for keeping the list up to date. Our [systems and account ownership register](/templates/account-register/) can help.

## Make sure the church controls each account

Check that each account is registered to the church. Your domain name and website are especially important, because losing them can take your email offline too.

We recommend that you:

- use a church email address for account recovery, not someone's personal address
- give at least 2 trusted people administrator access to each important account
- give everyone else only the access they need for their role

## Give each person their own login

Don't share logins. The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) says systems shouldn't require people to share accounts or passwords to do their job.

With individual logins, you can see who did what, and you can remove one person's access without affecting anyone else.

<Details summary="What to do if a service only allows one login">

Some services don't support individual logins. In these cases, we recommend that you:

1. Keep the login in a password manager that the church controls.
2. Share it with as few people as possible.
3. Turn on 2-step verification.
4. Change the password whenever someone with access steps down.

</Details>

## Make logins harder to steal

Follow the NCSC's advice for important accounts:

1. Use a passkey wherever it's offered. Passkeys are harder to steal through fake login pages than passwords or 2-step verification.
2. Where you still use a password, make it strong and use it only for that account. A password manager can help.
3. Turn on 2-step verification wherever passwords are still used. Any type is better than none.

<WarningText>

Change any password that has been reused on an important account as soon as you can.

</WarningText>

## Remove access when someone steps down

Add these checks to your process for when someone leaves a role:

- remove or disable their accounts
- remove them from shared files and mailboxes
- change the password on any login they could access

Review who has access to your important accounts every few months, as the NCSC recommends. A trustee or administrator could do this as a regular item in their calendar.
