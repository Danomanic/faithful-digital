Churches are targeted by scams and cyber attacks like any other organisation. Most attacks are simple, and a few habits will stop most of them.

The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) publishes free guidance for small organisations, including charities. This page summarises the steps that matter most for churches.

## Watch out for common scams

Common scams that target churches include:

- **Impersonation:** an email or text that seems to come from your minister or treasurer, asking someone to buy gift cards or make an urgent payment
- **Changed bank details:** a message from a "supplier" saying their bank details have changed
- **Fake login pages:** an email linking to a page that looks like your email or file sharing login

<WarningText>

Never pay money or change bank details because of an email or text alone. Check by phoning the person on a number you already have.

</WarningText>

Agree a rule that the church never asks anyone to buy gift cards or make urgent payments by message. Tell your congregation about it, so they can spot fake requests.

## Back up important information

Keep a copy of your church's important information in a separate place. This protects you from ransomware, accidental deletion and lost devices.

- check what your online services already back up, and for how long
- keep a backup of anything stored on a single computer
- test once a year that you can actually restore files from your backup

## Keep devices and software up to date

- turn on automatic updates for computers, phones and tablets
- replace devices that no longer get security updates
- use the built-in antivirus on Windows and Mac computers, and keep it switched on
- set a screen lock on every device that can access church accounts

If volunteers use their own devices for church work, ask them to follow these steps too.

## Protect your accounts

Strong logins are the most important protection. Read [set up accounts and logins](/guidance/accounts-and-logins/) for how to use passkeys, password managers and 2-step verification.

## Consider Cyber Essentials

Cyber Essentials is a government-backed scheme that checks you have basic protections in place. Most small churches don't need to be certified. Its checklist is still a useful guide, and some funders or partners may ask for it.

## If something goes wrong

1. Act quickly. Change passwords on any account that might be affected, and sign out other sessions.
2. If money was sent, contact your bank straight away.
3. Report fraud or cyber crime to Report Fraud, the national reporting service for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. In Scotland, contact Police Scotland on 101.
4. If personal data might be affected, follow your [data breach process](/guidance/data-protection/). You may need to tell the ICO within 72 hours.
5. Tell your trustees, and record what happened and what you've changed.

You can forward suspicious emails to the NCSC at report@phishing.gov.uk, and suspicious texts to 7726.
