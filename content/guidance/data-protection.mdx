Churches hold personal data about members, visitors, volunteers, children and donors. UK data protection law applies to churches of every size. This page covers the main steps, in a sensible order.

## Why churches need to take extra care

The UK GDPR gives extra protection to "special category data". This includes information about someone's religious beliefs and health.

Simply being on a church's contact list can reveal someone's religious belief. Prayer requests and pastoral notes often include health or family information. So a lot of what churches hold may count as special category data, which needs greater care.

## 1. Choose who leads on data protection

Ask one person to lead on data protection, and make sure the trustees know who it is. They don't need to be an expert, but they should have time to learn the basics and to keep records up to date.

Most small churches don't need a formal data protection officer. Check the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) guidance if your church carries out large-scale monitoring or processing of special category data.

## 2. List the personal data you hold

Make a simple list showing:

- what personal data you hold, for example the electoral roll, rotas, Gift Aid declarations or children's group registers
- why you hold it
- where it's kept, including paper records and personal phones
- who can see it
- how long you keep it

This list helps with every other step. Many churches find data they no longer need, such as old registers or spreadsheets on a former volunteer's laptop.

## 3. Check you have a lawful reason

You must have a lawful reason, known as a "lawful basis", for each use of personal data. Common ones for churches are:

- legitimate interests, for example keeping a contact list for church members
- legal obligation, for example keeping Gift Aid records for HMRC
- consent, for example putting someone's photo on your website

For special category data, you also need an additional condition. Churches can often rely on a condition for not-for-profit bodies with a religious aim, as long as the data is about members, former members or people in regular contact, and isn't shared outside the church without consent. The ICO's guidance explains this in detail.

## 4. Tell people what you do with their data

Write a privacy notice in plain English. Say what you collect, why, who you share it with, how long you keep it and how people can use their rights. Put it on your website and give it to people when you collect their details, for example on a welcome card.

## 5. Check whether you need to pay the data protection fee

Organisations that use personal data usually need to pay an annual fee to the ICO, unless they're exempt.

Many churches are exempt under the not-for-profit exemption. This only applies if you use personal data for your own members and supporters, and don't do things like run CCTV. Use the ICO's self-assessment tool on its website to check. If you do need to pay, charities only pay the lowest tier fee.

## 6. Keep data secure

- store personal data in church accounts, not personal ones
- give people access only to what they need for their role
- don't keep church records on personal phones or memory sticks
- lock away paper records, and shred them when no longer needed

Read [set up accounts and logins](/guidance/accounts-and-logins/) and [protect your church from scams and cyber attacks](/guidance/cyber-security/) for more.

## 7. Be ready to respond to requests and complaints

People have rights over their data. For example, they can ask for a copy of what you hold about them. You usually have 1 month to respond.

Since 19 June 2026, you must also give people a way to complain to you about how you use their data. You must acknowledge a complaint within 30 days and deal with it without undue delay. An email address in your privacy notice can be enough for a small church.

## 8. Know what to do if something goes wrong

A personal data breach could be an email sent to the wrong person, a lost phone or a hacked account.

<WarningText>

If a breach is likely to put people at risk, you must report it to the ICO within 72 hours of becoming aware of it.

</WarningText>

Record every breach, even if you don't report it, and note what you've changed to stop it happening again. Agree in advance who people should tell, so that no time is lost.
