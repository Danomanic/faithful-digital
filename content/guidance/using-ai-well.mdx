## Why we're hopeful, and why we draw lines

Artificial intelligence (AI) raises real questions for the church, and faithful Christians will reach different conclusions about them. We approach it with hope and with care.

We're hopeful because AI can lift burdens from people who are already stretched. It can give a volunteer back an evening, help a newcomer read a notice in their own language, or turn a dense report into something everyone can understand.

We're careful because of what we believe about people. Every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), and in Jesus, God came to us in person (John 1:14). Being known, prayed for and accompanied by another person is something no tool can provide. A chatbot can imitate understanding, but it can't love anyone.

Scripture encourages us to ask God for wisdom (James 1:5) and to test everything, holding on to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). That's the spirit of this guidance. We offer clear limits, along with our reasons for them, so that you can discern what's right for your church.

## Questions to discuss with your trustees

The Church of England's Ethical Investment Advisory Group sets out 5 principles for considering AI from a biblical perspective:

- flourishing as persons
- flourishing in relationship
- standing with the marginalised
- caring for the creation
- serving the common good

These can help your team ask better questions than "Will this save us time?". For example, does this use of AI respect people's skills? Could it leave someone out? Are we being honest about it?

## Where AI can help

Good uses usually involve low risk, no personal data and a person checking the result. They include:

- first drafts of notices, newsletters and routine letters
- summarising long public documents
- simplifying complex language
- translating public information, with a fluent speaker checking anything important
- improving accessibility, for example by drafting image descriptions or captions for checking
- generating ideas for events, teaching or worship
- help with spreadsheets and other routine admin

The person using AI remains responsible for the final result. Check facts, quotations and scripture references, and make sure the tone suits your church. AI tools can produce confident answers that are wrong.

## What we recommend keeping human

We recommend that churches don't use AI to:

- give pastoral care or spiritual direction, or respond to someone in distress
- make decisions about safeguarding, employment or people's roles
- answer pastoral questions through an automated chatbot
- replace prayer, study and discernment in preparing to teach or preach

Using AI as a research aid or to test an outline is a different question from having it write a sermon. Churches will draw that line in different places, so we suggest deciding on it together and being open with your congregation.

## Keep personal data out of AI tools

<WarningText>

Don't put personal, pastoral or safeguarding information into an AI tool your church hasn't approved for that purpose.

</WarningText>

Many AI tools can store or reuse what people type into them. The United Reformed Church's guidance lists information that shouldn't be entered, which we recommend adopting as a starting point:

- names or contact details of staff, volunteers or members
- safeguarding information or concerns
- pastoral conversations or sensitive personal details
- health, HR or financial information
- confidential church business or committee discussions

Churches need to take particular care under data protection law. The UK GDPR gives extra protection to personal data revealing religious beliefs. Information that links someone to your church may therefore need this higher level of protection.

<InsetText>

A simple test from the United Reformed Church: if you wouldn't share the information with a stranger, don't put it into AI.

</InsetText>

## Be open about how you use AI

Be honest when AI has made a meaningful contribution to significant work, such as a report, policy, public communication or material used in worship. Label AI-generated images, and don't use AI to make something appear real when it isn't.

Check for bias as well, because AI tools can repeat unfair assumptions found in the data used to build them.

## Agree who is responsible

Trustees remain responsible for decisions made with AI or informed by it. As the Diocese of York's advice for PCCs suggests, a small church may only need a conversation and a shared understanding of the risks. A larger church, with more staff and volunteers using AI, is more likely to benefit from a written policy.

Whichever you choose, your trustees should agree:

1. Which AI tools people can use, and for what.
2. What information must never be entered.
3. Who checks AI-assisted work before it's published.
4. Who people should ask when they're unsure.
5. When you'll review this, as the tools change quickly.
