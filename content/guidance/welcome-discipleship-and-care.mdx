Jesus told a story about a banquet where the host sent his servants out into the streets and lanes, and then further still, so that the house would be full (Luke 14:21–23). The church has always been sent outwards to find people and bring them in.

Today, many of those streets and lanes are online. Someone thinking about faith will often look at a church's website or social media long before they walk through the door. Others stay connected to their church through a phone, because illness, work or caring responsibilities keep them at home.

Digital tools can't do the inviting for us. They can make sure the door is easy to find and the welcome is real.

## Welcome starts before Sunday

For a newcomer, the first visit can take courage. They want to know simple things: what time to arrive, where to park, what happens to their children, what to wear, whether they'll be asked to do anything.

When a church answers those questions clearly, it's saying "we were expecting you". When the information is out of date or hard to find, it can feel like a closed door, however warm the people inside are.

This is one of the most loving things a volunteer with a laptop can do. Keeping service times, contact details and a few friendly photos up to date is quiet, faithful work that serves people the church hasn't met yet.

## Community between Sundays

The first believers met in the temple courts, but they also shared meals in their homes, prayed together and cared for one another every day (Acts 2:42–47). Church was never meant to happen only once a week.

Group messages, shared calendars and online prayer lists can help that everyday life of the church to flourish. A prayer request can reach the people who'll pray within minutes. A home group can stay in touch through a difficult week. A rota can be shared without a dozen phone calls.

The letter to the Hebrews urges believers to spur one another on and not to give up meeting together (Hebrews 10:24–25). Good tools serve that encouragement. They should draw people towards each other, and not replace being together.

## Reaching those who can't be there

For someone who is housebound, in hospital or caring for a relative, a recording of Sunday's service can be a lifeline. It keeps them within the worship of their church family and reminds them that they haven't been forgotten.

Jesus said that when we visit the sick, we visit him (Matthew 25:36–40). Technology doesn't replace that visit. Yet it can carry the life of the church into the room between visits, and give people something to talk about when someone does call round.

## Discipleship that goes with people

Discipleship happens in the ordinary hours of the week as much as on a Sunday. Sermon recordings, reading plans and short devotional messages can travel with people to work, on the school run or in a sleepless hour.

We'd encourage churches to see these tools as a way of extending conversations, not ending them. The deepest growth still happens as people read scripture together, pray together and follow Jesus alongside others who know them well.

## One body, many parts

Paul describes the church as one body with many parts, each needed and each honoured (1 Corinthians 12:12–26). Digital work is an opportunity for people whose gifts might otherwise go unnoticed.

The teenager who understands social media, the retired engineer who enjoys sorting out a spreadsheet, the person who loves to write: each can serve the church in a real way. We'd encourage leaders to notice these gifts, to thank people for them, and to make sure no one carries the work alone.

## Keeping it personal

The apostle John wrote that he had much to say, but would rather not use paper and ink, because he hoped to visit and talk face to face so that their joy would be complete (2 John 12). He used the tools available to him, and still longed to be present.

That's a good guide for us. We use digital tools gladly, for welcome, care and growth. We also remember that they serve something greater: a community of real people who belong to each other and to God.
